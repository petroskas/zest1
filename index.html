About epilepsy

A neurological condition. Anyone can have it.

Epilepsy is a neurological condition of the brain, not a curse, not witchcraft and not contagious. Seizures are short episodes of sudden, abnormal electrical activity in the brain that can change movement, awareness, sensation or behaviour. Having one seizure does not always mean epilepsy; epilepsy is diagnosed when seizures keep recurring.

The Zimbabwe Epilepsy Survivors Trust is run by and for people with lived experience. Together with partners such as St Daniels Rehabilitation & Vocational Training Centre, we replace fear and stigma with knowledge, dignity, skills and access to care — because lives matter and potential is real.

  • Career guidance, education and peer networking
  • Disability awareness & epilepsy aid training

Know the signs

Common seizure types and what they look like

Seizures are not all “falling and shaking”. Select a seizure type to watch its video illustration.

Whole brain

Tonic-clonic (convulsive)

  • Body stiffens, then jerks rhythmically
  • Loss of awareness, may cry out or lose bladder control
  • Usually 1–3 minutes; deep tiredness afterwards
Whole brain

Absence

  • Blank stare, fluttering eyelids, “switching off”
  • Lasts 5–20 seconds, no fall; common in children
  • Often mistaken for daydreaming
One area of the brain

Focal aware / impaired awareness

  • Twitching of one arm or face, odd smells, déjà vu, fear
  • Impaired: confused, lip-smacking, fumbling, wandering
  • Can spread to a convulsive seizure
Whole brain

Myoclonic

  • Sudden, brief jerks of arms, legs or body
  • Person stays aware; often on waking
  • May make people drop things
Whole brain

Atonic (“drop attacks”)

  • Sudden loss of muscle tone; head drops or person falls
  • Very brief, recovers quickly
  • High injury risk, so a helmet may be advised
Whole brain

Tonic

  • Sudden stiffening of muscles, often in sleep
  • Usually under a minute
  • May cause a fall if standing

Education

Causes, triggers, healthy habits and safety everywhere

What causes epilepsy?

  • Head injury, including birth injury or lack of oxygen at birth
  • Brain infections: meningitis, cerebral malaria, neurocysticercosis (pork tapeworm)
  • Stroke, brain tumour, or other brain damage
  • Genetic and developmental conditions; family history
  • In about half of people, no clear cause is found

Common seizure triggers

  • Missed medication (the most common cause of breakthrough seizures)
  • Lack of sleep and exhaustion
  • Stress, strong emotions, illness or fever
  • Alcohol, drugs and withdrawal
  • Flashing lights (only some people, “photosensitive epilepsy”)
  • Missed meals, dehydration; hormonal changes for some women

Triggers differ for each person. A diary helps find yours (see the Seizure diary below).

Habits that support seizure control

  • Take medicine at the same time every day; never stop suddenly
  • Sleep 7–9 hours and keep regular hours
  • Eat regular meals and drink water
  • Manage stress: talk, pray, walk, breathe
  • Avoid alcohol and drugs; attend clinic reviews
  • Never swim, cook on open fire or climb alone unless a doctor says it is safe

Seizure at home

  • Cushion the head, clear hard objects, time it
  • Keep cooking fires and boiling water away from the person; use raised cooking stands where possible
  • Prefer showers to baths; keep bathroom doors unlocked
  • Family members should learn first aid and keep medicine visible

Seizure at school

  • Teachers: stay, time it, protect the head, turn on the side after shaking
  • Do not send the child home alone; let them rest afterwards
  • Share a care plan with the school; classmates should be taught it is not contagious
  • Allow catch-up on missed lessons and exams

Seizure at work

  • Employers: know first aid, keep a calm space to rest
  • Guard people away from machines, heights and heat during a seizure
  • Colleagues should not restrain or give food or drink until fully alert
  • Epilepsy is not a reason for dismissal: make reasonable adjustments

Seizure in public

  • Move the crowd back; ask someone to call for help if needed
  • Keep away from roads, water, crowds and sharp objects
  • Do not give food, drink or tablets until fully awake
  • Call an ambulance if over 5 minutes, repeated, injured, or first seizure
  • Stay until the person is fully alert; speak kindly

General education based on guidance from WHO, ILAE and epilepsy foundations. Not medical advice.

    Seizure first aid

    Stay. Safe. Side.

    What to do when someone has a seizure.

    1. STAY with the person, stay calm and time the seizure.
    2. SAFE – keep them safe by clearing sharp or hard objects away.
    3. Cushion the head with something soft.
    4. Do NOT restrain them and do NOT put anything in their mouth.
    5. SIDE – once it is safe and shaking stops, gently turn them onto their side.
    6. Stay with them until fully alert; speak gently.
    1. Stay with the person and speak calmly.
    2. Guide them away from danger (roads, water, heat).
    3. Don't restrain them or shout.
    4. Reassure them as they come round; explain what happened.
    • A seizure lasts longer than 5 minutes
    • One seizure follows another without recovery
    • The person is injured, pregnant, or has trouble breathing
    • It is their first ever seizure

    Get to the nearest clinic or hospital as soon as possible.

    Frequently asked questions

    Clear answers about epilepsy and disability

    What is epilepsy?

    Epilepsy is a neurological condition of the brain. It is diagnosed when seizures keep recurring; one seizure on its own does not always mean epilepsy.

    Can I catch epilepsy from another person?

    No. Epilepsy is not contagious. Stay calm, protect the person from injury and follow the first-aid guidance on this site.

    What should I do when someone has a seizure?

    Stay with the person and time the seizure. Cushion their head, move nearby hazards and do not restrain them or put anything in their mouth. Call emergency services if a seizure lasts 5 minutes or more, repeats without recovery, causes injury or affects breathing.

    Can epilepsy be treated?

    Many people control seizures with prescribed medicine. Treatment differs for each person. Do not start, change or stop medicine without advice from a qualified health professional.

    Can a person with epilepsy go to school or work?

    Yes. People with epilepsy can study and work. Reasonable adjustments, an agreed seizure first-aid plan and an inclusive environment can help.

    What does an accessible and inclusive service do?

    It respects each person's choices, removes communication and physical barriers, offers reasonable accommodations and does not discriminate because of disability.

    Where can I read trusted disability and epilepsy resources?

    Use the online disability and epilepsy reading resources below, including Zimbabwean law, the UN disability convention and WHO epilepsy information.

    Can I share my story anonymously?

    Yes. The story form can store a story for review without displaying your name. Avoid putting identifying details into the story itself. Email and WhatsApp replies can reveal your contact details to ZEST.

    Empowering children with disabilities
